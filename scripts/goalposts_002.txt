## The Goalposts Never Stop Moving

You know the feeling. You agree to something clear and specific, and two weeks later the request has quietly changed.

This video breaks down why that pattern feels so destabilizing, what research says about it, and what you can actually do the next time it happens.

In this video, you will learn three facts about moving goalposts and one practical playbook for handling them.

First, a definition. Psychologists describe this as shifting standards: the terms of an agreement change after you have already accepted them.

The mechanism is simple but effective. When the rules move after commitment, your mind re-opens a decision you believed was closed, and each revision costs you another round of attention, time, and trust.

According to a 2016 study published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, participants who renegotiated fair deals reported lower trust even when the final outcome was identical. [[SOURCE: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology | The goalpost effect | 2016 | Correlation only: it does not prove intent]]

For example, imagine a coworker asks you to cover a shift on Friday. You agree. On Wednesday, the request is now the whole weekend, and somehow your original yes is treated as binding for the new version.

But be careful before you assume malice. Alternative explanations are common. The coworker may simply be overwhelmed, the schedule may have changed for operational reasons, or the request may reflect poor planning rather than strategy.

If you have felt that slow burn of a moving target, it makes sense. Many people feel exactly this and blame themselves first.

Here is the warning sign to watch: the size of the request changes only after you commit. That is the fingerprint of goalpost movement, not ordinary schedule chaos.

So what can you do? Use the playbook.

Step one: notice. Ask yourself one question. Is the request changing?

Step two: regulate. Don't answer immediately.

Step three: verify. Ask for the original agreement. Restate what you both accepted.

Step four: boundary. State what you agreed to, in writing if needed.

Step five: build. Continue only if the agreement is respected.

One limitation deserves honesty. Most of this evidence is correlational. Researchers cannot say why the rules moved, only that the pattern reliably damages trust.

To recap: notice the shift, regulate before responding, verify the original terms, state your boundary, and build only on respected agreements.

You do not need to win every negotiation. You just need the terms to stop moving.
